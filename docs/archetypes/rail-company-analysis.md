# Business archetype of a national rail company selling tickets to individual passengers

Source of archetype definitions: Neal Cabage, *Business Model Archetypes*, https://nealcabage.com/business-model-archetypes/

## Answer

The national rail company is a **Service** business. When a passenger buys a ticket, they are paying to be carried from A to B; the ticket is only the proof of that right. The value comes from the company running trains: drivers, crews, dispatchers and maintenance teams.

## How I tested each archetype

Cabage defines each archetype by two things: a **key activity** (what the business does to create value) and a **way of earning money** (what the customer pays for). For each archetype I asked:

1. What is the passenger actually buying when they buy a ticket?
2. Does the rail company's main work match the archetype's key activity?
3. Does the way fares are priced match the archetype's way of earning money?

## Analysis per archetype

| # | Archetype | Cabage's definition | Analysis | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Product** | One-time purchase of an artifact; key activity is product development | Buying a ticket feels like buying a product: one payment, fixed price, self-service. But the ticket has no value of its own, unlike an app or an ebook. It is worthless without the journey, and the company's main work is running trains, not developing products. | **NO** |
| 2 | **Service** | Doing something for the customer and charging a fee; paid for time and materials | The company does a job the passenger can't do alone: carrying them. People and company assets do that work, and it is used up as it is delivered. The fare depends on distance, time and class, so the passenger pays for the work actually done. It is a highly standardized service, but still a service. | **YES** |
| 3 | **Trade** | Connecting buyers and sellers; earns by buying low and selling high; key activities are sourcing and advertising | The company doesn't buy capacity from someone else to resell at a markup. It produces the capacity itself, so there's no resale margin. | **NO** |
| 4 | **Subscription** | Product + Service with ongoing access for a recurring payment | Season tickets exist, but the stated main activity is single tickets: one payment per trip. It would be YES only if passes were the main model. | **NO** |
| 5 | **Brokerage** | Trade + Service: trading on behalf of clients for a base fee plus commission | The company sells its own transport, not someone else's. Ticket resellers and travel agents are brokers; the rail operator isn't. | **NO** |
| 6 | **Marketplace** | Trade + Product: a self-service platform that recruits vendors and takes a commission per sale | The ticket shop sells only the company's own trips; no outside vendors sell through it. It would be YES only if it became a Trainline-style platform selling other operators' tickets. | **NO** |
| 7 | **Ecosystem** | Product + Service + Trade together, with partners and platforms | Within the stated scope, only one of the three basic archetypes (Service) is present, and there are no partner or platform layers. | **NO** |

## The judgment call: Product vs Service

The one close call is Product versus Service, because buying a ticket looks like a product purchase. It's still Service because what is delivered is the journey, not the ticket. Ticket sales are the sales channel for the service, not the product itself.
